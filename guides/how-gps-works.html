How GPS Works: Satellites, Signals and Your Phone

GPS feels like magic — tap a button and a dot appears on a map — but it is really just very precise timing. The system is built on a fleet of about thirty satellites orbiting Earth at roughly twenty thousand kilometres up. Each satellite broadcasts a radio signal stamped with the exact time it was sent, using an atomic clock accurate to billionths of a second. Your phone listens to those signals and works out how long each one took to arrive.

Distance is the key. Radio signals travel at the speed of light, so if a signal took a fraction of a second to reach you, your phone can calculate how far away that satellite is. With one satellite, you know you are somewhere on a huge sphere around it. With two, the possibilities narrow to a circle. With three, they narrow to two points. With four, the maths pins down a single point — and also corrects for the fact that your phone's clock is far less accurate than the atomic clocks in space. That is why a GPS fix needs at least four satellites, and why more satellites mean a better fix.

Your phone does not talk back to the satellites. GPS is a one-way system: the satellites broadcast, and billions of receivers listen silently. That is why GPS works anywhere on Earth with a clear view of the sky, from a desert to the middle of the ocean, and why it cannot be overloaded by too many users. It is also why GPS itself uses no mobile data — the positioning is free, though downloading the map around your position usually needs a connection.

Modern phones combine GPS with other systems. The American GPS network is joined by Russia's GLONASS, Europe's Galileo, and China's BeiDou, and most new phones listen to several at once, which means more satellites in view and a faster, more accurate fix. Assisted GPS, or A-GPS, uses the mobile network to tell the phone roughly where the satellites are, cutting the time to first fix from minutes to seconds. Wi-Fi and Bluetooth beacons fill in the gaps indoors, where satellite signals cannot reach.

GPS is not perfect. Signals bounce off buildings, weaken under trees and roofs, and can be deliberately jammed or spoofed in conflict zones. A phone in a pocket or a car park will always be less accurate than one held up in open sky. And GPS tells you where you are, not where you are going — your direction comes from the compass and from watching your position change over time, which is why a sat-nav sometimes points the wrong way for the first few metres of a journey.

Despite those limits, GPS is one of the most remarkable public systems ever built. It guides aircraft, ships, and emergency services, timestamps financial transactions, and sits in every smartphone on the planet. The next time your map snaps to your location in seconds, spare a thought for the atomic clocks twenty thousand kilometres overhead, quietly broadcasting the time so your phone can work out exactly where you are.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does GPS use mobile data?

No. GPS positioning is free and works offline. Only the map tiles and traffic data need an internet connection.

Why does GPS take a while the first time?

The phone needs to download satellite positions. A-GPS speeds this up using the mobile network; without it, a cold start can take a few minutes.

How many satellites do I need for a fix?

At least four for a full three-dimensional fix. More satellites in view generally means better accuracy.
