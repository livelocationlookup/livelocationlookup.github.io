Latitude and Longitude Explained in Simple Words

Latitude and longitude are the two numbers that describe every place on Earth. Think of them as the address system for the whole planet. Latitude tells you how far north or south you are from the equator, and longitude tells you how far east or west you are from the prime meridian in Greenwich, London. Together they form a pair of coordinates that can point to a single building, a park bench, or a spot in the middle of the ocean, with no street name needed.

Latitude runs from 90 degrees north at the North Pole to 90 degrees south at the South Pole, with the equator at zero. Lines of latitude are parallel circles that get smaller towards the poles. Longitude runs from 180 degrees west to 180 degrees east, meeting at the international date line in the Pacific. Lines of longitude are half-circles that all meet at the poles. A coordinate is usually written as latitude first, then longitude — for example, 51.5074, -0.1278 is central London, and 24.8607, 67.0011 is Karachi.

Coordinates are written in two common formats. Decimal degrees, like 51.5074, are the simplest and the format most apps and websites expect. Degrees, minutes and seconds, like 51° 30′ 26″ N, is the older format you will still see on paper maps and in some navigation equipment. One degree is divided into sixty minutes, and each minute into sixty seconds. You can convert between the two with a simple calculation, or with our coordinates tool, and it is worth knowing which format a device or form expects before you type numbers in.

Why do coordinates matter in everyday life? They are the language that maps, sat-navs, delivery apps and emergency services all share. When you drop a pin and send it to a friend, you are really sending coordinates. When a rescue team needs your position in the mountains, coordinates are more reliable than a description of the nearest landmark. They also work anywhere on Earth, including places with no addresses at all — a desert, a forest trail, or the open sea.

Reading coordinates is easier than it looks. The first number, latitude, tells you the north-south position: positive means north of the equator, negative means south. The second number, longitude, tells you east-west: positive is east of Greenwich, negative is west. The more decimal places, the more precise the point. Two decimal places get you within about a kilometre; four get you within about ten metres; six get you within about ten centimetres, which is more precision than most phones can actually measure.

Next time you see a pair of coordinates, try pasting them into a maps app or our coordinates-to-map-link tool and see where they land. You will quickly develop a feel for the numbers: latitudes near zero are tropical, longitudes near zero run through western Europe and Africa, and large negative longitudes belong to the Americas. It is a small piece of knowledge that makes maps, travel and technology noticeably easier to use.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which comes first, latitude or longitude?

Latitude always comes first in the standard format, then longitude. Remember it as lat-long, like going up the ladder before across.

How accurate are coordinates from a phone?

Usually within five to ten metres outdoors. Indoors or between tall buildings the error can grow to fifty metres or more.

What is the difference between GPS coordinates and an address?

An address depends on local street names and postal systems. Coordinates are universal and work anywhere on Earth, even where no address exists.
