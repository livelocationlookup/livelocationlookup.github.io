Emergency Location: How to Share Where You Are When It Matters

In an emergency, knowing how to share your location can save precious minutes. Whether you are injured on a trail, your car has broken down on a quiet road, or you need an ambulance at a place with no clear address, your phone can tell rescuers exactly where you are — if you know how to use it. The time to learn is now, not in the middle of a crisis.

The simplest method is your coordinates. Open your maps app and look for the blue dot; long-pressing it usually shows your latitude and longitude. Read those two numbers out to the emergency operator, or use our live location tool to copy them and your plus code. A plus code is even easier to read aloud than coordinates, and it points to a square about fourteen metres across — precise enough for an ambulance to find your door. If you can send a message, a dropped pin or a map link is better still, because it removes any chance of a misheard number.

Modern phones also help automatically. On both iPhone and Android, calling emergency services can send your location to the dispatcher through a system called Advanced Mobile Location. It activates only during an emergency call and sends a highly accurate position, often within a few metres. You do not need to do anything to use it, but it only works if location services are switched on — one more reason to keep them enabled.

There are situations where a call is not possible. If you have no signal for a voice call, a text message may still get through, because texts need far less signal. Save the habit: when travelling in remote areas, tell someone your route and your expected return time before you go. If you are overdue, they can raise the alarm with a starting point and a direction. Hikers and drivers in rural areas should also consider carrying a personal locator beacon, which sends a distress signal by satellite where no phone network exists.

For children and vulnerable adults, a family safety app can be a lifeline. Apps that share location within a family group let a parent see where a child is without the child needing to do anything in a moment of panic. Set these up together, explain what they do, and agree on when they are used. The goal is safety, not surveillance, and that distinction matters — especially with teenagers, who need to understand and accept the arrangement for it to work.

Finally, keep the basics ready. Know your home address by heart, including the postcode. Save your plus code somewhere easy to find. Keep your phone charged when travelling, and carry a small power bank on long journeys. In an emergency, the difference between a vague description and an exact location can be the difference between a quick rescue and a long search. Take five minutes today to find your coordinates and your plus code — it is a small preparation that you may one day be very glad you made.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should I tell emergency services about my location?

Your coordinates or plus code, plus any landmark or road name. A dropped pin or map link sent by message is even better.

Does my phone send my location automatically when I call for help?

On most modern phones, yes — Advanced Mobile Location sends an accurate position during emergency calls, if location services are on.

What if I have no phone signal?

A text message may still work with a very weak signal. In truly remote areas, a satellite personal locator beacon is the reliable option.
