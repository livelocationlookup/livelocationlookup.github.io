Plus Codes Explained: Addresses for Places Without Street Names

Billions of people live in places without a proper street address. Their homes sit on unnamed lanes in sprawling neighbourhoods, or in villages where directions are given by landmarks — turn left at the big tree, second house past the mosque. Deliveries fail, ambulances get lost, and registering for services becomes a struggle. Plus codes, also known as Open Location Codes, were created by Google to solve exactly this problem: a short, free code that works as an address for any place on Earth.

A plus code looks something like 8F2C+Q2P Karachi, or in full, 8F2C2Q2P+Q2P. The first part narrows the location down to a region, and the second part narrows it to a small square about fourteen metres across — roughly the size of a house. The plus sign separates the two parts, and the final letters can be replaced by a nearby town name, which makes the code shorter and easier to remember. Anyone with the code can paste it into Google Maps and be taken to the exact spot.

Plus codes are built directly from latitude and longitude, so they never run out and they never need a central authority to hand them out. Two places can never share the same code, and the code for a place never changes. That makes them very different from postal codes, which cover whole areas, and from what3words, a commercial system that assigns three random words to each square. Plus codes are open source and free forever, which is why they are used by postal services and emergency responders in several countries.

Using a plus code is simple. Our plus code finder tool generates one from your current coordinates or from any latitude and longitude you enter. Share it the way you would share an address — in a message, on a form, or on a delivery note. The person receiving it pastes it into Google Maps, and the map opens at the exact location. No street name, no landmark, no confusion. For a home business, a farm, or a house on an unnamed lane, a plus code can be printed on a card or painted on a wall.

The system also helps in emergencies. If you are injured on a hiking trail or broken down on a rural road, your coordinates or plus code tell rescuers exactly where you are, far more reliably than describing the nearest village. Many emergency apps now show your plus code on screen for this reason. It is worth generating yours at home and saving it somewhere safe, so you have it ready when it matters most.

Plus codes will not replace street addresses where they already work well, but for the huge number of places they do not, they are a quiet revolution. They cost nothing, work everywhere, and turn any spot on the planet into an address you can share. Frequently Asked Questions

Are plus codes free to use?

Yes. Plus codes are open source and free forever. Anyone can generate or use them without a licence or an account.

How accurate is a plus code?

A full plus code points to a square about fourteen metres across, which is precise enough to identify a single house or entrance.

Do plus codes work offline?

Yes. They are calculated from coordinates, so any app that can work out your position can generate one without a connection.
